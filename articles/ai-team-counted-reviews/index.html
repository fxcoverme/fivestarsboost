I Run a One-Person Company. My AI Team Counted the Reviews of 259 Stores, One by One.

FiveStarsBoost · November 2026

I run a one-person company in Ohio. It's called FiveStarsBoost: I reply to online reviews for local businesses too busy to do it themselves.

For a local store, reviews are the storefront. A bad review sitting unanswered makes everyone who finds that store afterward wonder whether the owner even cares. My job is to make sure every review gets a real answer within one business day.

When you run a company alone, the scarcest thing is never the product. It's customers. I had the service, the website, the pricing page — but nobody knew I existed. So I did what everyone does: buy a list, grab a template, blast it out.

Then I shut the blast down. This is about the slow way we replaced it with — and the first real reply it finally produced.

First, the ugly number: 280 sent, 0 replies

Before the change, we sent 280 cold emails from a generic template — subject lines like "Improve your Google rating," the kind of email you delete without opening.

Real replies: zero.

Why? A store owner gets pitched constantly, and my email had nothing to do with his actual store. It said "your reviews matter," but that was empty talk — I had never actually looked at his reviews.

So I told the team to stop. New rule: before we say a word to a store, we read its reviews. One by one.

The slow way, in five steps

Step 1: Build the list. We pulled together a master list of small businesses across all 88 counties in Ohio — 1,915 of them. Restaurants, dental practices, salons, plumbers. 98.6% have a public phone number.

Step 2: Verify every store by hand. This is the slowest step, and the most important. The AI team opened each of 259 candidate stores on Google Maps, sorted its reviews newest first, and counted: of the 10 most recent reviews, how many had the owner never replied to?

It has to be newest first, not totals. A store can show 500 reviews and a 4.8-star average and look healthy — but if 6 of its last 10 reviews are unanswered, the owner has stopped paying attention, and an unanswered review is the owner's real pain, sitting in public. You don't have to guess at it. You can count it.

Step 3: Segment, and only email the ones who need it. Of the 259 stores, 169 had 3 or more unanswered reviews in their last 10. Those 169 were the only ones we emailed. The other 90 were handling reviews fine; we left them alone.

Step 4: Write each email for one store only. No two subject lines were the same. Each one carried that store's real number: "7 of your last 10 Google reviews have no reply." And the body had one simple hook: I've already written replies to your 3 most recent unanswered reviews — reply "send" and I'll send them to you, free.

That shrinks the decision: not "should I buy this service?" but "do I want to see three replies already written for me?" — and it proves I actually looked at his store.

Step 5: Cap the volume, keep the discipline. Maximum 40 emails a day. Numbers only ever come from actual counts — never estimated, never made up. Bounced addresses get logged and are never emailed again. Follow-ups are a short new note a few days later, based on a fresh look — not the same email nagging twice. And the moment a human replies, that store comes out of every sequence immediately. Don't keep robo-mailing someone who answered you.

The first real reply came from a dental practice

From early November through today, the slow way has sent about 120 personalized first-touch emails and more than 100 follow-ups.

On November 13, the first real reply arrived. A dental practice in Las Vegas wrote back three words: "Like to see it."

Going from 0 to 1 is harder than going from 1 to 100. The slow way works — it's just slow, slow enough that by email 50, then 80, you start doubting yourself. But if the numbers are real and the email was written for that one store, eventually somebody looks back at you.

That same day, the team delivered. We opened the practice's Google page: 4.6 stars, 363 reviews — and reading newest first, 9 of the last 10 reviews had no reply. Then we wrote replies to its 3 most recent unanswered reviews, each one from the actual words in that review.

One was a long 1-star complaint accusing the practice of overcharging — the hardest kind to answer. Our rules: don't admit fault, because a public admission accepts the accusation. Don't argue, because fighting a customer in the review section scares off everyone reading it later. Do three things only: express regret, show you take it seriously, and invite the person to call the office and settle the bill offline.

We sent the 3 finished replies plus a checkup of its review page — the same free review checkup any business can request at fivestarsboost.com/checkup — free, as promised, no pitch stuffed in. Delivering what you promised comes first.

The lesson: what my AI team is never allowed to decide

The difference between 280 emails with 0 replies and the first real reply isn't how well the AI writes. It's what the AI is doing. Before, I had it "writing like a human." Now I have it doing the verification work humans are too lazy to do. So the team works under hard rules:

Every number must be measured. Whatever we counted on the screen is what we say. If we couldn't verify it, we write "not verified." Never a guess.

Every reply is written from the actual review text. If the customer complained about the wait, the reply doesn't say "thanks for visiting." Customers smell a copy-paste brush-off instantly — and a fake reply is worse than no reply.

Sequences stop the moment they should. A reply, an unsubscribe, a bounce — the sequence stops, and it gets logged. The scariest thing about automation isn't that it's slow. It's that it can't stop.

Everything leaves a record. When each store was checked, what the count was, when the email went out, what came back. If anyone questions a number, I pull the original record.

And the final call is still mine. Whether to send, what to say, which store is worth following up — the AI brings me the plan, I approve it. Running a one-person company doesn't mean handing it to AI. It means finally having a team that never gets tired.
